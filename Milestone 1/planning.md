**We chose two scholarly sources to reinforce our topic research project.** <br>
Scholarly Context <br>
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13548565251320755 <br>
Liu and Taneja (2025, Convergence) take BTS as a case study and ask how a genre treated as niche in the U.S. ended up with mainstream visibility. Their answer is not that American taste suddenly changed. It is that Billboard's ranking system, specifically the Social 50 chart and the Top Social Artist Award, flattens worldwide social engagement into a single ordinal number. That aggregation erases differences in regional audience size and consumption culture, so certain regional fanbases end up over-represented in what the chart claims is a global measure. The authors call this "audience mis-aggregation." This is the strongest source for our group's argument because it shows meaning being constructed by the measurement itself, not by the music. A chart position looks like a neutral fact about popularity, but it is an artifact of how the metric was designed. It also gives us a concrete way to talk about the BTS streaming controversy without taking a side on whether ARMY was "cheating." The chart was always constructing an audience, and fans just learned to read the construction. <br><br>

https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/soin.12470 <br>
Van Venrooij, Miller, and Schmutz (2022, Sociological Inquiry) analyze 47,230 critic reviews of 3,252 albums from Metacritic between 2000 and 2007 to test whether race shapes how critics assign genre. They find critics are less likely to describe Black musicians as genre-spanning than white musicians, and that multiracial groups get the most ambiguous genre identities. The takeaway is that genre categories are not just descriptions; they are judgments made by gatekeepers, and those judgments follow racial lines even when nobody involved intends them to. This supports the categorization half of our project and gives us a defensible way to frame the "Asian Pop" question. Instead of arguing about whether a separate K-pop category is racist or appreciative, we can ask the empirical version of the question: who gets sorted into a bounded ethnic or regional category, and who gets to be read as crossing categories? It also gives us a method we can borrow. They coded reviews for category-spanning language, and we could do a smaller version of that with award nominations or Spotify genre tags. <br><br>

https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20563051221094769 <br>
Analyzes how a protest song’s original political meaning gets decontextualized as it spreads through social media, such as TikTok challenges. In this journal, it shows one viral protest song and codes how people actually used it. The song started as a tool of resistance. On TikTok, it turned into lip-synced dance clips, hashtag trends, and comedic memes. The political message mostly disappeared. This gives our project a real example of meaning eroding on a platform. It also gives us a method for sorting sample videos into use categories that we can reuse for our dataset. <br><br>

https://archive.org/details/musicineverydayl0000deno/page/n8/mode/1up <br>
This scholarly source asserts that music’s meaning isn't fixed to the song itself. It gets built and rebuilt depending on where and how people use it. The author backs this up with real-world studies, such as an aerobics class and karaoke nights. This connects directly to our group’s theme. DeNora’s aerobics and karaoke cases are examples of people using the same music differently for different bodily and emotional effects depending on the setting. <br><br>
